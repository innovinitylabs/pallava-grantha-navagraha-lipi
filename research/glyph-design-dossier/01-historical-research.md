### 2. Indian Jyotiṣa & Astronomical Notation Traditions

#### 2.1 The Absence of Standalone Single-Character Planetary Glyph Systems
In Western astronomy, individual abstract symbols for planets date back to Byzantine and medieval astrological codices (e.g., ☉, ☽, ♂, ♃), derived from Greek abbreviations and divine attributes.

In contrast, classical Indian mathematical astronomy (Siddhānta) and practical calendar calculation (Pañcāṅga) developed along different typographic trajectories:

1. **Scribal Abbreviation Ligatures (Akṣara-saṅketa):**
   In South Indian palm-leaf and paper manuscripts (written in Grantha, Tamil, or Malayalam scripts), scribes typically indicated planets by writing the initial syllable of the planetary name:
   - *Cū* (சூ) for Sūrya / Cūriyan.
   - *Can* (சந் / திங்) for Candra / Cantiran / Tiṅkaḷ.
   - *Cev* (செவ்) for Maṅgala / Cevvāy.
   - *Pu* (பு) for Budha / Putaṉ.
   - *Kuru* (குரு) or *Viyā* (வியா) for Guru / Bṛhaspati / Viyāḻaṉ.
   - *Cuk* (சுக்) or *Veḷ* (வெள்) for Śukra / Cukkiraṉ / Veḷḷi.
   - *Ca* (ச) for Śani / Caṉi.
   - *Rā* (ரா) for Rāhu / Rāku.
   - *Ke* (கே) for Ketu / Kētu.
   These were not standalone universal symbols; they remained script-dependent phonetic abbreviations. When transliterated into another script, the characters changed entirely.

2. **Alphanumeric Number Notations (Āryabhaṭa & Katapayādi):**
   In theoretical treatises (*Āryabhaṭīya*, *Sūryasiddhānta*, *Mahābhāskarīya*), planetary revolutions, orbital periods, and sine tables were encoded using vowels and consonants assigned numerical values:
   - The **Āryabhaṭa system** assigned numbers to consonant-vowel combinations to express enormous orbital figures in metrical verse.
   - The **Katapayādi system** (widely used in Kerala and Tamil astronomical schools) mapped consonants to digits from 0 to 9.
   These systems were computational and mnemonic, not visual iconographic symbols.

3. **Horoscopic Kundali Charts & Mandalas:**
   Planetary positions were mapped spatially within 12-house rectangular or diamond-shaped charts (such as the South Indian square chart divided into 12 rāśi compartments). Planets were noted inside the compartments using numbers or script abbreviations, not distinct abstract symbols.

**Conclusion for Fable Designers:** There is no ancient, universally accepted Indian equivalent of the Western single-character planetary glyph set. Nava Graha Lipi is creating this symbol system for the first time, deriving its visual authority from Tamil-region epigraphical ductus and iconographic traditions rather than falsely claiming to revive an extinct script symbol set.

### 3. Navagraha Visual & Symbolic Traditions

#### 3.1 Stone Iconography in the Tamil Region
The worship and artistic depiction of the Navagraha reached its architectural maturity in South India under the Pallava and Chola dynasties:

1. **Pallava Period (6th–9th Century CE):**
   - Earliest rock-cut relief panels depicting the planets in a horizontal frieze (sapta-graha or navagraha) are attested in northern Tamil Nadu (e.g., Mahabalipuram, Tiruttani, and Kanchipuram region).
   - In early Pallava iconography, the Grahas were frequently depicted standing in a line or seated in meditation, holding water vessels (*kamaṇḍalu*), rosaries (*akṣamālā*), or weapons.
   - Sūrya is typically elevated, holding two blooming lotuses (*padma*), wearing a cylindrical crown (*karaṇḍa makuṭa*), and accompanied by an aura of radiance (*prabhāmaṇḍala*).
   - Chandra holds a water pot or lotus bud, crowned with a crescent motif.
   - Rāhu is depicted as a colossal head with wide eyes and a serpent's hood, or as a bust without a torso.
   - Ketu is depicted with a human torso terminating in a coiled serpent tail or comet-like appendage.

2. **Chola Period & Standardized Navagraha Pīṭha (9th–13th Century CE):**
   - By the 10th century, Tamil temple architecture established the independent Navagraha platform (*Navagraha Maṇḍapa*).
   - A raised square stone pedestal (*pīṭha*) with Sūrya situated in the absolute center, elevated above the others.
   - The remaining eight Grahas are arranged along the perimeter facing specific cardinal and intermediate directions:
     - East: Śukra (facing East or West depending on temple tradition).
     - South-East: Maṅgala (facing South).
     - South: Guru (facing North).
     - South-West: Rāhu (facing North-East).
     - West: Śani (facing West).
     - North-West: Ketu (facing South-West).
     - North: Budha (facing South).
     - North-East: Chandra (facing South-East).
   - Critical rule: In standard South Indian temple tradition, no two surrounding Grahas face each other directly; each is aligned according to cosmological cardinal directions.

### 5. Pallava & Grantha Inscriptional Visual Characteristics

The historical script traditions of the Tamil region provide the specific anatomical language for Nava Graha Lipi.

#### 5.1 Pallava Grantha Epigraphy (4th–9th Century CE)
Pallava Grantha is the primary script used by the Pallava dynasty to engrave Sanskrit inscriptions on rock-cut temples, stone steles, and copper plates in Kanchipuram, Mahabalipuram, Tiruchirappalli, and surrounding regions.

Key calligraphic characteristics documented in Pandey (2018, L2/18-083) and Burnell (1878):

1. **Horizontal Serifs & Head-marks:**
   - Unlike Devanagari's continuous top hanging line (*shirorekha*), Pallava characters possess isolated, elegant top serif marks (*head-marks* or *notches*).
   - In monumental stone carvings (e.g., Rajasimha's Kailasanatha temple, Kanchipuram), these head-marks terminate in subtle chisel flares.
   
2. **Parabolic, Sweeping Curvature:**
   - Curves in Pallava letterforms are organic and dynamic rather than rigid circular arcs.
   - Downward strokes frequently sweep outward before resolving into inward loops, creating a sense of buoyant monumental grace.

3. **Open-Counter Loop Ductus:**
   - Characters such as *KA*, *RA*, *MA*, *BA*, and *LA* feature loops formed by continuous counter-clockwise motion.
   - These loops maintain substantial internal negative space, preventing visual clogging when carved in granite or engraved on copper.

4. **Stout, Grounded Baseline Stability:**
   - Vertical stems ground firmly on the baseline. Characters rarely feel top-heavy; the center of visual mass is held slightly below the mid-point.

#### 5.2 Grantha Development (7th Century–Present)
As Pallava Grantha evolved through the Chola (10th–13th century) and Vijayanagara/Nayaka periods into modern liturgical Grantha, certain changes occurred:
- Characters became rounder and more cursive, adapting to palm-leaf writing with iron styli (*ezhuthāṇi*).
- Loops became tighter and more circular.
- Horizontal serifs softened into continuous curved arches.
- *Nava Graha Lipi prioritizes the archaic, stone-carved Pallava ductus over late cursive Grantha, preserving monumental structural clarity.*

#### 5.3 Parallel Influence: Vatteluttu (5th–13th Century CE)
- **Vatteluttu** (literally "round script") was the primary historical script used for writing Tamil in the southern Pandyan and Chera realms.
- Characterized by fluid, circular, uninterrupted ductus with few sharp corners.
- Offers valuable inspiration for calligraphic flourishes, continuous spirals, and non-linear terminal sweeps (especially for Candra, Śukra, and Rāhu).

#### 5.4 Archaic Context: Tamil-Brahmi (3rd Century BCE–3rd Century CE)
- The earliest attested script in the Tamil region, found on cave beds and pottery (Mangulam, Pugalur, Kodumanal, Keeladi).
- Characterized by stark geometric simplicity, open linear skeletons, and archaic dot marks (*puḷḷi* / *bindu*).
- Informs the geometric foundation: the central bindu of Sūrya, the stark apex of Maṅgala, and the purity of linear proportions.
